1 Crystal Growth
The quality of a solid-state experiment is inseparably linked to the quality of the crystal under
investigation. Whether one aims to determine a lattice structure from diffraction data, measure
charge carrier mobility in a transport setup, or resolve vibrational modes by spectroscopy, all of
these rely on materials with high purity and low defect density. Poorly grown samples broaden
diffraction peaks, obscure electronic states, and diminish transport properties. In many research
programs, sample preparation is the true bottleneck: no sample, no science.
Impurities and defects are unavoidable in real crystals, but their concentration and distribution
depend strongly on the growth route. Impurities (foreign atoms) may enter during synthesis from
the growth chamber, the growth atmosphere, chemical transport agents, or incomplete purification of
the source material. Defects (such as vacancies, dislocations, grain boundaries) are generated when
the lattice fails to propagate perfectly during solidification, or when thermodynamic driving forces at
elevated temperature favor a finite defect population. Growth parameters—temperature gradients,
pulling and translation speeds, atmosphere, pressure, and cooling or annealing protocols—are chosen
to minimize these imperfections or to drive them out after growth.seed

Bulk crystal growth
Czochralski (CZ) method. A charge of the material is melted in a crucible, and a seed crystal
is dipped into the melt and slowly pulled upward while rotating (Fig. 1a). Solidification proceeds
at the solid–liquid interface, replicating the crystallographic order of the seed. The crystal diameter
is controlled by a balance of heat flow and pulling rate; rotation helps homogenize the melt. CZ
excels at producing large single crystals (e.g. silicon, germanium) and underpins wafer production
in microelectronics. Technical challenges include thermal stresses that can nucleate dislocations,
and contamination from the crucible (e.g. oxygen uptake from quartz in Si). Post-growth anneals
and carefully tuned thermal gradients are employed to reduce defect densities and to control oxygen
precipitation in downstream processing.

Bridgman–Stockbarger method. Here, the material is sealed in an ampoule and translated
slowly through a well-defined temperature gradient so that the charge melts and then resolidifies
from one end to the other. Ideally a single seed determines the crystallographic orientation and the
solidification front remains planar. The method is mechanically simpler than CZ and is widely used
for compound semiconductors, e.g. GaAs, CdTe. Because the melt is confined, interactions with
container walls can introduce impurities. Segregation during solidification may yield compositional
inhomogeneities if the translation speed and gradient are not optimized. Grain formation is sup-
pressed by maintaining a stable interface and modest growth rates; too steep a gradient or too fast
a pull promotes polycrystallinity and dislocation generation.
Flux growth. Flux growth exploits a high-temperature solvent (the “flux”) in which the target
compound has appreciable solubility. Upon slow cooling, the solution becomes supersaturated and
the compound crystallizes out, ideally on a seed or as free-standing single crystals (Fig. 1b). Because
dissolution and precipitation occur well below the melting point of the compound, flux growth is
especially valuable for materials that decompose or melt incongruently. Choice of flux is central: a
self-flux (one component of the target stoichiometry) minimizes chemical contamination, whereas
foreign-metal fluxes (e.g. Sn, Bi) can widen the accessible solvent window but risk incorporation or
surface wetting. In practice, a charge composed of starting elements (or pre-reacted powders) and
flux is sealed in a crucible, heated to homogenize, then cooled at very slow cooling rates (typically
0.1 − 0.5 K/h) through the supersaturation regime.
Chemical Vapor Transport (CVT). Chemical vapor transport grows crystals by reversible
gas-phase reactions that shuttle material from a hot source zone to a cooler sink zone within a
sealed ampoule (Fig. 1c). A small amount of transport agent X (e.g. halogens or halides) reacts
with the source compound, denoted AB, at temperature Thot to form volatile species AX(g) and
B(g) into the gas phase; these diffuse to the cold zone at Tcold, where the equilibrium shifts and
the target compound AB re-precipitates in solid form, releasing the transport agent X into the
gas phase. The net flux is set by the gradient in chemical potential between zones and the gas-
phase diffusivity. CVT is particularly effective for layered chalcogenides and many transition-metal
compounds, where direct melt growth is impractical or yields polycrystals. Both flux growth and
chemical vapor transport achieve equilibrium crystal growth far below the melting point, which
improves chemical homogeneity at the expense of growth speed.
Thin-film growth
Many materials and device structures used in modern technology cannot be grown as large bulk
crystals but instead require atomically precise thin films or heterostructures. These are obtained by
epitaxial growth techniques, in which atoms or molecules condense on a single-crystalline substrate
and arrange themselves in registry with its lattice. The term epitaxy (from Greek epi, “upon”,
and taxis, “arrangement”) emphasizes that the crystallographic orientation of the film is determined
by the substrate. Depending on the source of the depositing species, epitaxy can be realized in
ultra-high vacuum (molecular beam epitaxy, MBE) or in a chemically reactive gas environment
(metal–organic chemical vapor deposition, MOCVD). Thin-film growth unites thermodynamics and
kinetics: surface energies decide whether atoms wet or cluster, while lattice mismatch and strain
determine how many coherent layers can be sustained. Techniques such as MBE and MOCVD
translate these principles into atomic-scale control, enabling modern semiconductor technology and
the exploration of quantum phenomena in engineered heterostructures.

Molecular Beam Epitaxy (MBE). In MBE, beams of atoms or molecules are evaporated from
effusion cells under ultra-high-vacuum conditions (p ≲ 10−8 mbar) and directed toward a heated
substrate (Fig. 2a). Because the mean free path is much larger than the chamber dimensions, the
particles travel ballistically, allowing excellent control over flux and composition. Layer thickness
is determined by the arrival rate and substrate temperature, and can be monitored in real time by
electron diffraction methods.
The low background pressure minimizes contamination, leading to extremely pure interfaces and
sharp heterojunctions. However, maintaining lattice matching between film and substrate is essential
to prevent misfit dislocations. For small mismatches, strain can be elastically accommodated over
several monolayers; beyond a critical thickness, plastic relaxation occurs and dislocations nucleate
to relieve the strain energy. Precise flux control and growth interruptions at submonolayer level
enable the fabrication of quantum wells, superlattices, and two-dimensional electron gases central
to modern semiconductor physics.
Metal–Organic Chemical Vapor Deposition (MOCVD). MOCVD achieves epitaxial growth
through chemical reactions of volatile precursor molecules at a heated substrate in a controlled atmo-
sphere (typically 10−1–102 mbar). Organometallic compounds such as trimethylgallium or diethylz-
inc deliver the metal component, while hydrides such as AsH3 or PH3 supply nonmetals. At the
substrate temperature (600 ◦C to 1000 ◦C), these precursors decompose, releasing the constituent
atoms, which incorporate into the growing film while gaseous by-products are pumped away.
Compared to MBE, MOCVD operates at higher pressures and is less demanding in vacuum tech-
nology, making it suitable for industrial-scale wafer growth. It remains the standard for compound
semiconductors such as GaAs, InP, and GaN, used in LEDs and high-frequency electronics. The
principal challenges lie in controlling gas-phase reactions, precursor depletion, and uniformity across
large substrates. Because chemical reactions can generate carbon or oxygen residues, purification of
precursors and precise temperature control are critical for achieving low defect densities.

As the film becomes thicker, the elastic energy stored in the lattice increases proportionally to
the film thickness, because each additional layer adds more strained material. Eventually, it
becomes energetically favorable for the film to relax by forming small lattice imperfections at
the interface, such as dislocations or stacking faults. The film thickness at which this happens
is called the critical thickness hc.

To get a feeling for typical numbers, consider GaAs grown on an AlAs substrate, which has

hundred nanometers thick can be grown without strain relaxation. In contrast, for GaN grown
on sapphire, the mismatch is about |ε| ≈ 0.04 (4%), and the corresponding critical thickness
is only a few nanometers. Beyond that, misfit dislocations form to relieve the strain, leading
to mosaicity and reduced carrier mobility.
In practice, modern epitaxy exploits this interplay between strain and relaxation. For ex-
ample, thin strained layers can be used deliberately to tune band gaps and enhance carrier
mobility (“strain engineering”), while thicker films require graded buffer layers or van der
Waals substrates to avoid defect formation.

2 Real-Space Probes I: Scanning tunneling and atomic force
microscopy
Optical microscopy is limited in resolution by the wavelength of light: even under ideal conditions,
features smaller than roughly λ/2 (a few hundred nanometers) cannot be resolved. Real space atomic
details of surfaces remained inaccessible until the 1980s, when a new class of instruments—scanning
probe microscopes—overcame this limit by exploiting interactions between a sharp tip and a surface
at the atomic scale.
The first of these was the scanning tunneling microscope (STM), invented by G. Binnig and
H. Rohrer in 1981. It provided the first direct images of individual atoms on a solid surface and
earned its inventors the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics. A few years later, the related atomic force
microscope (AFM) extended the idea to non-conductive samples, making scanning probe techniques
central to modern nanoscience.

An alternative is the constant-height mode, in which the tip height remains fixed while variations
in tunneling current are recorded. This mode is faster but riskier—any protrusion higher than the
set distance can cause a tip crash. Constant-height operation is therefore restricted to atomically
flat surfaces such as graphite or semiconductor terraces.
The quality of an STM image depends critically on the sharpness and cleanliness of the tip. In
practice, tips are usually made from tungsten or platinum–iridium wires a few tenths of a millimeter
thick. Tungsten tips are often prepared by electrochemical etching, in which a short wire is immersed
in a sodium hydroxide solution and biased against a counter electrode. When the wire thins to
a fine neck, it breaks spontaneously, leaving a sharp apex with a radius of curvature of only a
few nanometers (Fig. 3b). The freshly etched tip is rinsed, sometimes annealed in vacuum, and
occasionally further sharpened by field evaporation or gentle indentation into a clean metal surface.

3 Real-Space Probes II: Transmission Electron Microscopy
After exploring scanning probe methods that map surfaces atom by atom, we now turn to a technique
capable of imaging within solids. The transmission electron microscope (TEM) provides real-space
information from thin crystalline specimens and can reach sub-˚angstr¨om resolution, sufficient to
resolve individual atomic columns deep in the bulk. Whereas STM and AFM are limited to the
outermost atomic layers, TEM reveals the internal structure, defects, and interfaces of materials.
Principle of operation
The concept of using electrons instead of light for microscopy dates back to the early 1930s. Because
electrons exhibit wave–particle duality, Ernst Ruska realized that their wavelength could be much
shorter than that of visible light, and that electromagnetic lenses could be used to focus them just as
glass lenses focus light. Together with Max Knoll, he built the first transmission electron microscope
in 1931 and demonstrated a spatial resolution surpassing optical microscopes. For this pioneering
work, Ruska was later awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986—the same year that Gerd Binnig
and Heinrich Rohrer received the prize for inventing the STM. Both breakthroughs transformed our
ability to “see” matter on the atomic scale, one through electron optics, the other through quantum
tunneling.
In a TEM, a high-energy electron beam—typically 80 keV to 300 keV—is generated by a thermionic
or field-emission gun and guided down an evacuated electron optics column. A series of electro-
magnetic condenser lenses shape and focus the beam onto the sample, typically a thinned slab of
crystalline material. The sample must be sufficiently electron-transparent (50 nm to 100 nm) so
that a significant fraction of the electrons can traverse it without complete absorption. After in-
teraction with the sample, the transmitted and scattered electrons are refocused by the objective
and projection lenses to form a magnified image or diffraction pattern. In former days the pat-
tern was detected by a fluorescent screen, whereas nowadays modern semiconductor cameras with
few-electron sensitivity are well established.
Interaction of electrons with matter. When electrons penetrate a crystalline solid, they scatter
with both atomic nuclei and the surrounding electron cloud:
• Elastic scattering: the electron changes direction but not its kinetic energy. It arises from
the Coulomb potential of the atomic cores and is responsible for diffraction and image contrast.
The angular distribution of this scattering carries structural information about the arrange-
ment of atoms. At high accelerating voltages, the elastic mean free path (typically 50 nm to
200 nm)is long enough for thin specimens to transmit a measurable fraction of the incident
beam.
• Inelastic scattering: the electron transfers energy to the sample, exciting vibrations (phonons),
collective excitations (e.g. plasmons), or ionizing electrons. This process leads to energy loss,
heating, and beam damage, and limits the total dose a specimen can tolerate. Inelastic scat-
tering also reduces image contrast by adding background intensity.
The requirement of thin samples therefore stems from the balance between these two processes:
the crystal to be imaged must be thick enough to represent the bulk but thin enough that most elec-
trons experience at most one significant scattering event before leaving the sample. This competition
between scattering and absorption governs both image contrast and achievable thickness.

Electron optics and image formation. he principles of electron optics closely mirror those of
geometric optics. Just as in an optical microscope, image formation in a TEM follows the principles of
geometric optics. Magnetic lenses—coils of wire producing an axially symmetric magnetic field—act
on moving electrons via the Lorentz force, bending their trajectories toward the optical axis much
like a convex glass lens focuses light rays. A typical TEM employs several such lenses in sequence:
the condenser lens controls the beam convergence and illumination area, the objective lens forms
the first magnified image of the specimen, and the intermediate and projector lenses further enlarge
this image onto the viewing screen or detector (Fig. 5a). The overall magnification is approximately
the product M = MobjectiveMintermediateMprojector. Since the individual magnifications are each

Numerical aperture and collection angle. In analogy to optical microscopy, the resolution of
a TEM also depends on the range of scattering angles collected by the objective lens, often expressed
as a numerical aperture (NA). Formally, NA = n sin α, where α is the semi-angle of the objective
lens aperture and n the refractive index of the medium (unity in vacuum). Because the electron
wavelength is extremely small, even a modest angular aperture of a few milliradians corresponds to
very high resolving power. However, increasing α also enhances lens aberrations and reduces contrast
by admitting more highly scattered electrons. In practice, the objective aperture therefore acts as a
spatial filter: by selecting a smaller collection angle, one emphasizes mass–thickness and diffraction
contrast, whereas a larger aperture favors high-resolution, phase-contrast imaging. Thus, while the
NA in TEM formally plays the same role as in light optics, its optimization reflects a compromise
between resolution, contrast, and aberration control rather than simple diffraction limits.

HAADF-STEM imaging
Whereas conventional TEM relies on interference between transmitted and diffracted beams, scan-
ning TEM (STEM) analyzes electrons scattered at defined angles, providing new types of image
contrast. TEM forms images using the entire transmitted beam (bright-field) or selected diffracted
beams (dark-field). In modern STEM instruments, the beam is instead focused into a fine probe
and raster-scanned across the sample. Detectors placed at different scattering angles collect the
transmitted electrons to form images with various contrast mechanisms.
A particularly powerful mode is the high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) STEM, where elec-
trons scattered to large angles (tens of milliradians) are recorded by an annular detector (Fig. 5a).

Sample preparation
Achieving high-resolution imaging requires electron-transparent specimens. Bulk samples are thinned
mechanically to a few tens of micrometers, then ion-milled or etched to perforation. For site-specific
studies, a focused ion beam (FIB) microscope can lift out and thin cross-sectional lamellae directly
from devices or heterostructures. Typical final thicknesses are 30 nm to 80 nm, striking a balance
between minimizing multiple scattering and maintaining structural integrity. Surface contamination
and beam damage are minimized by gentle low-energy cleaning immediately before insertion into
the TEM.
